Organizational Cognition

1. A Convergence Without a Cause

Something strange is happening in enterprise AI.

Anthropic builds a protocol for connecting AI to every data source inside a company.

OpenAI builds the same thing, but calls them Connectors and ships them inside ChatGPT.

Microsoft weaves Copilot into the Microsoft Graph: the connective tissue of every Office document, email, and meeting inside an enterprise.

Google does the same with Gemini and Workspace.

Palantir builds an entire operating system around something it calls an ontology: a structured representation of how an organization works.

Cursor ships a .cursorrules file encoding a team's conventions into the coding agent; within a year, every rival tool reads the same format.

Glean, an enterprise search company, rebrands as an "AI platform" and builds connectors to hundreds of enterprise applications.

Salesforce rebuilds Slackbot into an agent running on a competitor's model, reaching thousands of applications over a competitor's protocol.

Block ships Buzz, where agents are not integrations but members, each with its own keypair and its own reputation.

These companies have different strategies, different distribution, different business models, and different founding philosophies. Their CEOs describe their roadmaps in different language. Their investors make different bets.

And yet their product roadmaps are converging on the same destination.

The convergence is not subtle. Memory. Connectors. Projects. Custom instructions. Agent frameworks. Enterprise identity. Governance layers. Every major AI platform is building the same set of capabilities, just with different names and different entry points.

Then the part that should stop you. Having built these layers, four of these companies handed them to a neutral foundation, governed jointly with the rivals who stood to gain most from their staying proprietary.

Existing explanations account for pieces of this. No single explanation accounts for all of it.

Which suggests we are missing a category.

2. Four Explanations, Four Gaps

Better Models

Where it works.

For two years, the dominant theory of competition in AI was straightforward: the company with the smartest model wins. This theory explained a great deal. It explained why billions flowed into training runs. It explained why benchmark leaderboards dominated technical discourse. It explained why enterprises evaluated AI providers primarily on model quality.

The theory made a prediction: as models improve, the gap between leaders and followers determines market outcomes.

Where it breaks.

The theory does not explain why every frontier lab, despite competing aggressively on model quality, is simultaneously building an identical set of infrastructure that has nothing to do with model architecture. If smarter models were sufficient, Anthropic would not need MCP. OpenAI would not need Connectors. Google would not need to embed Gemini in Workspace. Those products solve a different problem than intelligence.

The theory also does not explain why investment in the surrounding infrastructure is accelerating rather than concentrating on the model layer. If the bet were purely on intelligence, the marginal dollar would go into training runs, not into connectors and permission models.

A common version of this argument leans on model quality gaps compressing. I am leaving that out, because the measurements disagree: depending on which benchmark and which comparison you take, the gap between leaders and followers is narrowing, holding, or widening slightly. The argument does not need it. Even if the leaders were pulling away, it would not explain why the leaders are the ones building the surrounding infrastructure fastest.

Something else is driving the roadmap.

Better Workflows

Where it works.

A second theory holds that the value in enterprise AI is in automating workflows. Build an agent that can complete multi-step tasks, connect it to enterprise tools, and let it execute. This theory explains the rise of agent frameworks, the focus on tool use, and the emphasis on reliability. It explains why "agentic" became the industry's favorite adjective in 2025.

Where it breaks.

Workflows are valuable, but they are not sticky in a structural sense. A workflow encoded in one platform can be replicated in another. The harder problem is not what the AI does, it is how the AI knows what to do in a specific organizational context.

Two companies in the same industry can have identical workflows and produce different outcomes. The difference is not the workflow definition. It is the accumulated context that determines which workflow to invoke, when, with what parameters, and under whose authority.

Workflows explain execution. They do not explain judgment.

Better Distribution

Where it works.

Microsoft and Google have distribution advantages that no AI-native company can match. Copilot ships inside software that a billion people already use. Gemini is embedded in tools that enterprises have already purchased. This theory explains why Microsoft and Google can afford to be fast-followers on model quality: they win on distribution, not on intelligence.

Where it breaks.

Distribution explains how you reach an enterprise. It does not explain what happens after you arrive. If distribution were sufficient, Microsoft's first wave of Copilot features, which were essentially a chat interface bolted onto Office, would have created durable enterprise adoption. Instead, what followed was a rapid expansion into Graph-grounded responses, custom copilots, and agent capabilities. Microsoft is not just distributing AI. It is building infrastructure to ground AI in the specific context of each enterprise.

2026 supplied a cleaner version of the same test. From that summer, Slack ships bundled with every new Salesforce account, which is about as pure a distribution position as enterprise software offers.1 Salesforce spent it rebuilding Slackbot on a competitor's model, speaking a protocol it did not own, reaching for context held in thousands of other vendors' applications. A company that believed distribution was the prize would not spend its distribution wiring itself into everyone else's systems.

Distribution gets you in the door. It does not explain what you build once you are inside.

Statelessness

Where it works.

The fourth explanation is the most technically credible, and the one I find hardest to dismiss. Language models are stateless. Every request starts from zero, and the context window is finite. Anything the model needs to know has to be pushed into that window on every single call. Given that constraint, memory, connectors, projects, and custom instructions are not a new category of anything. They are the obvious engineering patches for statelessness: caches, retrieval layers, and prompt prefixes that reconstruct on each request what a stateful system would simply have retained.

This explanation is strong precisely because it is boring. It predicts the convergence without appealing to any shared intuition about the future of the enterprise. Every lab faces the same architectural constraint, so every lab builds the same compensating machinery, in roughly the same order, for roughly the same reasons. No new conceptual category is required. The constraint is enough.

Where it breaks.

Statelessness explains why you would want memory and connectors at all. It does not explain the shape of what is being remembered.

If the problem were purely a bounded window, the natural fix would be better retrieval over generic content: larger indexes, cheaper embeddings, smarter chunking, longer contexts. Some of that is happening. But the products are not converging on generic recall. They are converging on organization-specific judgment: which reviewer this routes to, what this team means by "done", which of two conflicting documents is authoritative, how much risk this company tolerates. None of that is a fact sitting in a repository waiting to be fetched. It has to be inferred from how the organization behaves, and then stored somewhere that is not the model.

The statelessness account also makes a prediction that is not holding. If surrounding infrastructure exists to compensate for a small window, then as windows grow, that infrastructure should taper. Windows have grown by orders of magnitude. The infrastructure around them has grown faster.

The clearest test arrived in July 2026, when MCP shipped the largest revision in its history and used it to become more stateless, not less. The 2026-07-28 specification replaced the bidirectional stateful session with a self-describing request/response model so that servers could run on serverless and edge infrastructure, and moved Tasks, the feature for managing long-running operations, out of the core protocol and into an extension.2 If the industry believed the surrounding machinery existed to give models memory, the shared protocol is where you would put that memory. Instead the protocol deliberately shed it.

Two different problems share a name. Statelessness at the transport layer is a scaling decision, and the answer to it is caching and routing. Statelessness at the organizational layer did not move when the protocol got simpler: the memory went up, into extensions, platforms, and products, which is where it had been heading all along.

And it is silent on the parts of the roadmap that are growing fastest. An audit trail for AI-made decisions. A permission model for which agent may act on whose authority. An approval path for changing a system prompt. None of these compensate for a finite context window. They are controls over judgment, and a perfectly stateful model with an unlimited window would need them just as much.

Statelessness explains the mechanism. It does not explain the content.

The Residual

Each theory explains part of the landscape. Better models explain why performance matters. Better workflows explain why agentic capabilities are valuable. Better distribution explains why incumbents have structural advantages. Statelessness explains why context has to be reassembled from the outside at all.

None of them explain why every player is converging on the same set of capabilities, memory, connectors, custom instructions, governance, regardless of whether they are a model company, a workflow company, or a distribution company. And none of them explain why the thing being assembled is so consistently organization-specific judgment rather than organization-specific facts.

The convergence suggests a common problem that predates any individual company's strategy.

3. Organizational Cognition

Definition

Organizational cognition is the collective capacity of an organization to perceive information, make decisions, coordinate action, and continuously learn, as expressed through the patterns that currently live only in human interaction, not in formal artifacts.

Every clause matters.

Collective capacity, this is not about individual intelligence. Organizational cognition is a property of the system, not its components. A company filled with brilliant people can have poor organizational cognition if information flows badly and decisions don't compound.

Perceive, decide, coordinate, learn, these are the four functions. Perception is what gets noticed. Decision is what gets chosen. Coordination is how work gets aligned. Learning is whether the organization gets better over time. All four are required. A company that perceives well but decides poorly is not cognitively healthy. A company that coordinates well but never learns is coasting on past decisions.

Expressed through patterns in human interaction, not formal artifacts, this is the boundary. If something can be written down in a document and executed by someone who has never worked at the company, it is knowledge. If it requires human judgment, unwritten norms, or tacit understanding to execute, it is cognition.

What It Is Not

This definition distinguishes organizational cognition from several adjacent concepts:

ConceptDistinction
KnowledgeKnowledge is what is known. Cognition is how it is used to decide. A knowledge base stores facts. Organizational cognition determines which facts matter, how they are weighted, and what to do when they conflict.
MemoryMemory, in the sense of raw retention of past states, is inert. Cognition is the processing of current states. An organization can remember every decision it ever made and still make the next one poorly. Deciding what to retain and surface is a different activity, and a cognitive one (see section 4).
IntelligenceIntelligence is raw capability. Cognition is applied capability within a specific context. A brilliant outsider with no organizational context has high intelligence and zero organizational cognition.
WorkflowWorkflow is a defined sequence of steps. Cognition determines which sequence to invoke, when to deviate, and how to resolve ambiguity that the workflow did not anticipate.
OntologyAn ontology is a structured representation of entities and relationships. Organizational cognition includes ontologies but extends to the processes that create, maintain, and act on them.
DocumentationDocumentation is explicit. Organizational cognition is largely implicit. You cannot read a company's Confluence and reconstruct how it actually makes decisions.

The test: if you can export it as a file, import it into another organization, and it produces the same decisions there, it is not organizational cognition. It is data.

The test needs a boundary condition, because externalization is not all-or-nothing. Parts of organizational cognition can be written down, and increasingly are. A style guide, a runbook, an escalation matrix: each captures a thin, static slice of judgment, and each is genuinely useful. What none of them capture is the process that produced them and keeps them alive: knowing which rule applies when two conflict, noticing that a rule has gone stale, deciding to break it this once and being right. The artifact is data. The ongoing activity of authoring it, revising it, and knowing when to deviate from it is cognition.

So the test is not a filter that rejects anything with a file extension. It is a way of asking how much of the live process a given artifact manages to carry, and every real externalization will be lossy in exactly this way. That matters later, when I get to .cursorrules, which looks at first glance like a direct counterexample to my own test.

Why Existing Terms Don't Capture It

"Institutional knowledge" comes closest, but it overemphasizes knowing and underemphasizes processing. An organization can possess vast institutional knowledge and still suffer from poor organizational cognition if that knowledge is not activated at the right moments, by the right people, in the right combinations.

"Company culture" captures some elements (risk tolerance, communication patterns) but misses others (approval paths, delegation norms, trade-off heuristics). Culture is a component of organizational cognition, not a synonym.

"Organizational intelligence" is sometimes used in management literature, but it typically refers to the aggregate IQ of the workforce, not the systemic capacity to process information and decide.

This is a genuinely new category, or rather, a category that has always existed but has been invisible because it had no technological substrate. It lived entirely in people. There was nothing to build for it, so there was nothing to name.

AI changes that.

4. A Taxonomy

Organizational cognition is not one thing. Breaking it into dimensions makes it possible to analyze any company's product strategy against a consistent framework.

The six dimensions below do not map one-to-one onto the four functions in the definition, and it is worth being explicit about that rather than letting the seam show. Reasoning is decide. Coordination is coordinate. Learning is learn. Memory is where perceive lands once you account for time, since perception is not only what an organization notices now but what it keeps and re-surfaces later. Delegation and Governance are not additional functions sitting beside the four. They are the layers above them: Delegation distributes the authority to perform the four, and Governance controls and audits how that authority is exercised and changed. I break them out because products are built against them separately, not because the definition acquired two extra limbs.

One more clarification, because section 3 explicitly ruled memory out. The dimension below is not retention. Storage is inert, and an organization that recalls everything can still decide badly. What the taxonomy calls Memory is the organizational process of curating retained context and deploying it at the moment of decision: what is worth keeping, what gets surfaced to whom, and what is allowed to fall away. Two organizations with identical archives can differ entirely on that, and the difference is cognitive.

4.1 Memory

What does the organization retain, and how is past context made available for future decisions?

This is the dimension most AI products have tackled first. Persistent chat history. Project-level context. The ability to recall previous interactions and decisions. But organizational memory is deeper than conversation logs. It includes which documents mattered, who was consulted, what objections were raised and resolved, what alternatives were considered and rejected.

4.2 Reasoning

How does the organization process information to produce decisions?

This includes decision-making heuristics, trade-off frameworks, risk tolerance thresholds, and escalation criteria. Two organizations with identical data and identical memory can reason differently because they weight factors differently. One engineering team treats latency as a hard constraint; another treats it as a target. Neither is documented. Both are organizational cognition.

4.3 Coordination

How is work decomposed, assigned, sequenced, and tracked?

This includes delegation patterns, authority structures, handoff protocols, and dependency management. It answers questions like: who needs to approve this? What needs to happen before this can start? What is the signal that this is done? Coordination is the most visible dimension of organizational cognition because it produces artifacts (tickets, PRs, status updates). But the rules that generate those artifacts are often unwritten.

4.4 Delegation

Who is trusted to do what, and under what constraints?

Delegation is distinct from coordination. Coordination is about sequencing work. Delegation is about granting authority. Every organization has implicit delegation norms: this person can approve spend up to X, this team owns decisions about Y, this kind of change requires broader review. These norms are rarely fully documented and often discovered through violation.

4.5 Learning

Does the organization's capacity to decide improve over time?

Learning is the meta-dimension. It is the organization's ability to update its own memory, reasoning, coordination, and delegation patterns based on outcomes. A postmortem that produces a new checklist is organizational learning. A postmortem that produces no change is not. The difference is not whether the postmortem was written. It is whether the organization's future behavior changed.

4.6 Governance

How are the other five dimensions controlled, audited, and evolved?

Governance is the dimension that determines who can modify organizational cognition, how changes are reviewed, and whether the system remains aligned with organizational objectives. It is the difference between "the AI learned from how we work" and "the AI is making decisions we cannot explain or override."

5. Reading the Roadmaps

If organizational cognition is a useful lens, it should make sense of product decisions that look unrelated under other frameworks. Here is what each company's roadmap reveals when read through this lens.

Anthropic

Anthropic's strategy is the most architecturally explicit. Claude Code encodes engineering team practices into version-controlled configuration files. Projects create persistent workspaces where organizational context accumulates. MCP is an open protocol for connecting AI to the data sources where organizational cognition is expressed (or latent). Memory persists across conversations.

Read through the organizational cognition lens, Anthropic is building the protocol layer. MCP is not a product feature. It is a bet that organizational cognition will need standardized interfaces: a way for AI to connect to the systems where decisions are documented, discussed, and executed. If the bet is correct, MCP becomes infrastructure. If it is wrong, MCP is a connector standard in a world that does not need one.

Anthropic's roadmap makes sense under the assumption that enterprises will treat their accumulated AI context as a durable asset that needs to be portable and interoperable. That is fundamentally an organizational cognition thesis, whether or not Anthropic uses the term.

Two things have happened since that are worth more than the rest of the roadmap combined. In December 2025 Anthropic gave MCP away, donating it to the Agentic AI Foundation, a directed fund under the Linux Foundation that it co-founded with Block and OpenAI, with backing from Google, Microsoft, AWS, Cloudflare and Bloomberg.3 Then in July 2026 the protocol shipped its fifth revision under that neutral governance, by which point it was running close to half a billion SDK downloads a month across its Tier 1 SDKs.2

Handing a bet to a foundation that includes your competitors is only rational if the bet was never on owning the interface. It is a concession that a connector standard controlled by one lab is worth less than a connector standard everyone trusts, which is a strange thing to concede about a product feature and an obvious thing to concede about plumbing. Anthropic appears to have decided that the value is not in the pipe. If organizational cognition is the asset, the pipe should be free, and whoever accumulates the most context through it wins anyway.

OpenAI

OpenAI's approach is more product-integrated and less architecturally explicit, but traces the same pattern. ChatGPT Memory persists user context across conversations. Connectors link ChatGPT to Google Drive, Microsoft OneDrive, and enterprise applications. Custom GPTs and Projects allow teams to create context-specific AI configurations. Deep Research and Operator extend the system from answering questions to taking actions, from perception and reasoning into coordination and delegation.

OpenAI's roadmap makes sense under the assumption that organizational cognition will accumulate primarily through direct use, people interacting with AI in the course of their work, rather than through explicit configuration. The connectors are not about data access. They are about grounding AI in the actual artifacts of organizational life.

In April 2026 OpenAI made that assumption explicit and retired the alternative. Workspace Agents replaced Custom GPTs outright for business, enterprise and education accounts, which are now on a deprecation path with no shutdown date yet named.4 The replacement is instructive in every detail. A Custom GPT was a configuration: you wrote instructions once and it followed them. A workspace agent is Codex-backed, runs in the cloud whether or not anyone is watching, carries memory scoped per user and per agent, is owned by a team rather than a person, can be corrected mid-conversation, and reaches into Slack and Salesforce to do work.

The word OpenAI's own materials use is that agents "improve over time." That is a claim about learning, not configuration, and it is the difference between a saved prompt and something accumulating. Notably, the memory is deliberately not pooled: it is per user and per agent, not one shared brain for the company. Something worth reading twice, given that a single organizational mind is the obvious thing to build if you believe the asset is organizational. OpenAI is betting the asset assembles from many small accumulations rather than one, which is a bet about how organizations actually work.

Microsoft

Microsoft's entry point is the Microsoft Graph: the existing representation of organizational relationships stored in email, documents, meetings, and Teams conversations. Copilot uses the Graph to ground responses in organizational context. Copilot Studio allows enterprises to build custom agents with organization-specific knowledge and behavior. The agent framework extends Copilot from a Q&A tool to an actor in organizational processes.

Microsoft's bet is that organizational cognition is already partially encoded in the Microsoft Graph, and that the path to capturing it is through the tools where it is already expressed. This is a distribution argument with a cognitive payload: the Graph is not just a data asset. It is a partial representation of how the organization works, who talks to whom, what documents matter, what decisions are being made.

At Build in June 2026 Microsoft stopped treating this as an implication of Copilot and shipped it as a named layer. Work IQ is described as an intelligence layer over Microsoft 365, and it is built, in Microsoft's own architecture diagrams, out of three things: Data, Memory, and Inference.5 Alongside it came Agent 365, which gives administrators central control over which MCP servers an agent may reach,6 and Entra Agent ID, generally available since April 2026, which issues agents first-class identities in the corporate directory rather than letting them borrow a human's credentials.7

I did not expect a vendor to name the layers this plainly, and it is worth being honest that a company arriving at Data, Memory, and Inference is convergent evidence for the taxonomy in section 4 rather than proof of it. Naming is cheap. What is not cheap is the shape of the governance products, and they are the tell. An identity system for non-human actors and an admin console for which tools they may call are not features you build for a chatbot. They are features you build once agents act on real authority and somebody has to answer for it. The industry numbers here are stark, and worth quoting with their disagreement intact: non-human identities already outnumber human ones somewhere between 45 to 1 and over 100 to 1 depending on whose survey you read and how they count, and roughly ninety per cent of organizations report no reliable way to know what their production agents are actually doing.8 The spread is wide enough that no single figure should be trusted. The direction is not in dispute by anyone.

Google

Google's strategy mirrors Microsoft's but with different assets. Gemini is embedded in Workspace. Vertex AI Agent Builder allows enterprises to construct agents grounded in their own data. NotebookLM is a curious but telling product: it is designed to help individuals and small teams reason over their own document collections: a lightweight form of organizational cognition for the personal and team scale.

Google's roadmap makes sense under the assumption that organizational cognition will be distributed across the tools where work happens (Gmail, Docs, Drive, Calendar) and that the AI layer needs to be present in all of them simultaneously. The bet is on pervasiveness rather than a central cognitive platform.

By 2026 Google had consolidated the scattered pieces. Agentspace became Gemini Enterprise, Vertex and Agentspace collapsed into one Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform, and Workspace picked up a layer Google calls Workspace Intelligence. Its framing of the problem is the sharpest sentence any of these companies has produced on the subject: today's workflows are information-rich but context-poor. That is the distinction in section 3 written by a marketing department. Underneath, Agent Engine Sessions and Memory Bank went generally available to give agents persistent context across interactions.9

Google's contribution to the picture is less about memory than about interoperability. Where MCP standardizes how an agent reaches a tool, Google's A2A standardizes how agents reach each other. Google donated it to the Linux Foundation in June 2025 as its own project,10 eighteen months before Anthropic routed MCP to a different Linux Foundation vehicle, and by its first anniversary it had over 150 supporting organizations and native integration in Azure AI Foundry, Amazon Bedrock AgentCore, and Google Cloud.11

Two rival companies, on separate timelines and through separate legal structures, each concluded that the layer they had built was worth more as a commons than as a product. Coordination and delegation, in the taxonomy's terms, are both getting wire formats, and neither is being sold. That is the behaviour of an industry racing to standardize the substrate so it can compete on what accumulates on top of it.

Palantir

Palantir is the most explicit about the concept, even if it uses different language. Its core product, the Ontology, is a structured representation of the entities, relationships, and decision rules that constitute an organization's operations. AIP (Artificial Intelligence Platform) layers AI on top of the ontology, allowing the system to reason over organizational structure, apply governance rules, and execute operational workflows.

Palantir's roadmap makes sense under the assumption that organizational cognition must be governed, that enterprises will not tolerate AI making decisions without auditable, controllable, and modifiable rules. This is the governance dimension made explicit. Palantir's challenge is that its model requires significant upfront investment in ontology construction, which limits its addressable market. But the thesis it embodies, that governed organizational cognition is the durable enterprise asset, is the same thesis the rest of the industry is converging on from different directions.

By 2026 Palantir had stopped describing the ontology as a data model and started describing it as a control plane: the thing that answers who authorized an agent's action, what that action cost, and whether its output can be trusted. The engineering follows the sentence. Management has discussed an agent engine SDK with cost attribution per agent, per session, and per workflow, administrative caps, and provenance tracing.12 Those are accounting primitives. You build them when agent decisions have to be explained to someone who did not make them.

The commercial signal is genuinely mixed, and it would be dishonest to file it as confirmation. Palantir raised full-year 2026 guidance to roughly $7.66 billion, about 71 per cent growth and its largest raise ever, while the stock fell around 35 per cent across the same stretch of the year.13 Both facts are real. The demand side says enterprises are buying governed agent infrastructure faster than expected; the market side says nobody is confident about what that infrastructure is ultimately worth or who ends up owning it. For a framework that predicts a category is forming, an unresolved valuation fight over the most explicit player in it is roughly what you would expect to see, which is also a reminder that the framework does not tell you which company wins.

Cursor

Cursor is a narrower but instructive case. It serves a single function (software engineering) for a single constituency (developers). Its .cursorrules file is a mechanism for encoding team-specific conventions, coding style, architectural preferences, library choices, testing expectations, into the AI coding agent. These are not facts. They are judgment. Two teams building the same product with the same requirements can have different .cursorrules and produce different code. The difference is organizational cognition at the team level.

This is where I owe the reader an answer, because .cursorrules appears to fail my own test. It is a plain text file. You can email it to another company in ten seconds. By the letter of the export test, that makes it data, and a framework that holds up its own counterexample as evidence is not a framework.

The boundary condition from section 3 is what does the work here. Send a team's .cursorrules to a different team and you have sent the rules without the reasons. The receiving team cannot tell which lines are load-bearing and which are leftovers, which were added after a painful outage and which were copied from a blog post, or when a rule should be suspended because the situation it was written for no longer holds. Drop one team's file into another team's repository and you will not get that team's code. You will get compliance with a snapshot. What did not travel is the loop that produced the snapshot: the friction that prompted each line, the review that accepted it, the judgment that overrides it in the cases it did not anticipate, the deletion six months later when it stopped being true.

The file is data. The loop is cognition. And the loop is what Cursor is actually positioned on, because the file is only ever revised from inside the editor where the work happens. That is the general shape of every externalization in this essay: a legible artifact sitting on top of an illegible process, useful in proportion to how much of the process it carries, and worthless the moment it is separated from it.

Cursor's roadmap makes sense under the assumption that even within a single function, organizational context matters enough to justify persistent configuration. If this is true for coding, it is likely true for legal review, financial analysis, hiring, and every other knowledge-work function. Cursor is the smallest legible instance of the broader thesis, which is what makes it a useful one: the unit is a team rather than an enterprise, and the artifact is a file you can read in a minute.

Since I first wrote this section, the industry has run the experiment for me. .cursorrules is no longer the interesting artifact, because the format got standardized out from under it. AGENTS.md, published by OpenAI in August 2025 and handed to the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation as a founding project alongside MCP, is now read natively by essentially every serious coding agent, Claude Code and Codex and Cursor and Copilot among them, and by community counts sits in tens of thousands of repositories.314 A file format for encoding team judgment went from one vendor's idea to vendor-neutral infrastructure in about a year.

This is the cleanest available test of the file-versus-loop claim, and it passes. If the artifact were the asset, standardizing it industry-wide would have flattened the differences between teams: same format, same tools, same instructions, same output. That is not what happened. Tens of thousands of repositories converged on one syntax and continued producing entirely different software, because what varies between teams was never the file. The format becoming a commons is the same move as MCP and A2A becoming commons, one layer down: nobody is defending the container, because the container was never where the value was. The loop that writes the file, argues about it, and deletes the line that stopped being true is still local, still unstandardized, and still the thing you cannot email anywhere.

Glean

Glean is the most interesting case because it started as enterprise search and is now building toward something else. Enterprise search solves a knowledge problem: find the document, the message, the person who knows. Glean's expansion into AI agents, custom apps, and workflow automation suggests a recognition that retrieval is not enough. Organizations need the system to do something with what it retrieves, to reason, to decide, to act.

Glean's roadmap makes sense under the assumption that enterprise search was solving the wrong problem, or at least an incomplete one. The valuable problem is not "find the information." It is "use the information to produce a decision." That transition, from knowledge retrieval to cognitive participation, is the same transition the entire industry is making.

The market has priced that transition. Glean raised a $150 million Series F at a $7.2 billion valuation in 2026 and reported roughly $300 million in ARR, up about 89 per cent year over year, having crossed 250 million agentic actions on the platform by January.15 It still describes its core asset the same way it always has: a permissions-aware knowledge graph across a hundred-plus enterprise applications.

That phrase is doing more work than it looks. A knowledge graph is the retrieval story. Permissions-aware is the cognition story, because it encodes who is allowed to know what, which is a fact about the organization's structure rather than about its documents. Glean's defensibility against Microsoft and Google was never going to be better search. It is that the graph accumulates, and a competitor starting today would have to re-derive it inside each customer. Whether that is a real moat or a temporary one is the open question for the whole category, and Glean is the purest place to watch it: a company whose entire valuation rests on accumulated context, with no model and no distribution to hide behind.

Slack and Block

The two most recent entrants matter because neither is a model company, and they arrive at the same place from opposite ends of the openness spectrum.

Salesforce spent 2026 rebuilding Slackbot from a joke into an agent. It reached general availability in January,16 then took on more than thirty AI features in March: meeting transcription across any video platform, a desktop agent that watches what you are working on, a lightweight native CRM, and, most tellingly, an MCP client that lets it orchestrate Agentforce, Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, Notion, Workday, ServiceNow and thousands of other applications. It runs on Claude. From summer 2026, Slack ships bundled with every new Salesforce account.1

Read as a product, this is an assistant. Read through this lens, Salesforce is claiming the coordination layer. Slack already holds the record of who asked whom for what, which decisions were made in a thread, and what "done" sounded like the last hundred times. That is the raw material of section 4.3, sitting in a product most companies already pay for. The company owning it does not need the best model. It needs to be the place where work is discussed, and to speak enough protocol to act on what it hears. That Salesforce chose someone else's model and someone else's protocol to do it with is the whole argument in one procurement decision.

Block took the opposite route in July 2026. Buzz is an Apache-2.0 chat workspace, self-hostable, model-agnostic, built on Nostr, in which agents are members rather than integrations. Every participant, human or agent, holds a cryptographic keypair that belongs to them and not to the platform, with its own permissions and its own activity trail, and that identity is portable: it travels to any compatible system carrying its history and reputation. Agents post, review code, and run automations the team has approved. Git hosting sits in the same window, though that part is early, and you can point it at Claude Code, Codex, or Block's own goose.1718

Buzz is early enough that both outlets covering it said so plainly, and it may well go nowhere. What makes it worth including anyway is that it is a direct attack on the assumption the rest of this essay treats as load-bearing, and it comes from a co-founder of the foundation that now governs MCP. Every other company here accumulates organizational cognition inside its own boundary, which is what makes it a moat. Block is arguing that agent identity, permissions, and reputation should be self-sovereign and portable by construction, so that the accumulated context belongs to the organization rather than the vendor. Section 7 predicts that switching costs rise until a shared representation emerges and fall afterwards; section 8's sharpest falsifier is an enterprise changing platforms without meaningful loss. Buzz is the first entrant deliberately trying to build the thing that would trigger both. It is worth watching not because it is likely to succeed, but because it is the rare case of a company attacking the moat rather than digging one.

The Pattern

Every one of these roadmaps makes sense if you assume the following:

  • Organizations generate decision-relevant patterns that are not captured in formal documentation.
  • Those patterns can be externalized through continuous interaction between people and AI.
  • Once externalized, those patterns become a durable organizational asset.
  • The asset becomes more valuable over time as it accumulates.
  • Switching AI providers means losing or rebuilding that asset.

If you do not assume these things, many of these product decisions look like unnecessary complexity. Why build MCP when APIs already exist? Why build Projects when chat works fine? Why build an ontology when a vector database stores everything?

The answer, consistent across every company's roadmap, is that these products are not solving for data access. They are solving for context accumulation.

6. What History Suggests

Every major computing revolution externalized something that previously lived only in human minds and human interaction.

Writing externalized memory. Before writing, knowledge existed only in individual minds and oral transmission. Writing created a persistent, shareable representation of what was known. It did not replace thinking. It externalized one component of cognition, memory, so that thinking could operate on a larger, more durable substrate.

Books externalized knowledge at scale. The printing press made externalized memory cheap and distributable. An idea recorded in Venice could reach a reader in London without passing through a chain of individual minds.

Spreadsheets externalized calculation. Before VisiCalc, financial modeling required manual computation. The spreadsheet did not eliminate judgment, it externalized the mechanical component of reasoning so that judgment could operate on a larger set of scenarios.

Databases externalized structured records. Before databases, organizational data lived in filing cabinets and individual recollection. The database made organizational facts queryable, shareable, and durable.

Cloud computing externalized infrastructure. Before AWS, compute and storage were physical assets managed by individual operators. The cloud externalized provisioning, scaling, and maintenance so that organizations could operate on a higher level of abstraction.

Each externalization had the same structure: something that previously required human attention and effort became a persistent, accessible, and improvable system. And each externalization created enormous economic value, not because it made the previous thing cheaper, but because it enabled new forms of work that were previously impossible.

The question is: what is AI externalizing?

If the answer is "organizational cognition", the patterns by which organizations perceive, decide, coordinate, delegate, learn, and govern, then the current moment is not about chatbots, agents, or model benchmarks. It is about the construction of a new layer of enterprise infrastructure that does for organizational decision-making what databases did for organizational records.

That is a much larger claim than "AI will automate workflows." It is also a claim that makes sense of the product convergence we observe.

The analogy cuts against part of my own argument, though, and I would rather say so than hope nobody notices. Every externalization on that list ended in standardization. Writing produced alphabets and grammars. Printing produced the codex and typesetting conventions. Spreadsheets produced a formula syntax so universal that a new product is judged by whether it opens someone else's file. Databases produced SQL. Cloud produced container images and provider-agnostic infrastructure definitions. In each case the substrate became portable, portability became commoditized, and whoever had bet on lock-in eventually lost that bet. That history supports the prediction that portability standards will become a battleground. It undercuts the prediction that switching costs will keep rising.

I think both can be true, but only in sequence, and only because this externalization has a property the others did not. The earlier ones standardized quickly because what was being externalized already had a formal shape before anyone externalized it. Double-entry bookkeeping was a formal system centuries before VisiCalc. Relational algebra was formalized before SQL existed to express it. Standardization in those cases meant agreeing on a syntax for semantics everyone already shared. Organizational cognition has no prior formalism. There is no agreed representation of what an approval norm or a risk threshold even is, and two vendors instrumenting the same company would not produce comparable artifacts. Until there is a shared semantics, an export format is a container with nothing standard inside it.

So the honest version of the prediction is narrower than "lock-in wins." Switching costs rise during the period before a shared representation exists, and fall once one arrives. The open questions are how long that period lasts and how it ends: the way it did for databases, where a formalism arrived and the moat evaporated, or the way it did for cloud, where portability was achieved at a layer below the thing anyone actually valued. My guess is that it lasts longer here than in any of the earlier cases, because the substrate is tacit judgment rather than a syntax, and because the governance layer will have to be standardized before the content can be. Nobody agrees on a wire format for decisions before they agree on who is allowed to make them. But I would not bet on lock-in as a permanent condition. Nothing on that list has been.

7. Evidence, Inference, Prediction

A framework is useful only if it distinguishes between what is observed, what is concluded, and what is anticipated.

Evidence

These are product decisions and public statements, not interpretations:

  • Anthropic released MCP as an open protocol for connecting AI to data sources (November 2024).
  • Anthropic shipped Claude Code with version-controlled project configuration (February 2025).
  • OpenAI shipped Connectors for Google Drive and Microsoft OneDrive in ChatGPT (March 2025).
  • OpenAI launched Projects in ChatGPT with custom instructions and persistent context (December 2024).
  • Microsoft embedded Copilot in Microsoft 365 with Graph-grounded responses (2024–2025).
  • Microsoft launched Copilot Studio for custom enterprise agents (2024).
  • Google integrated Gemini across Workspace and launched Vertex AI Agent Builder (2024–2025).
  • Palantir launched AIP with ontology-grounded AI operations (2023–2024).
  • Cursor shipped .cursorrules for team-level AI coding conventions (2024).
  • Glean expanded from enterprise search to AI agents and custom applications (2024–2025).

The 2026 additions matter more than the earlier ones, because they are the years in which the layer stopped being a set of product features and started acquiring standards bodies, identity systems, and accounting:

  • Google donated A2A, its agent-to-agent protocol, to the Linux Foundation (June 2025); it passed 150 supporting organizations and native support in Azure AI Foundry, Amazon Bedrock AgentCore and Google Cloud by its first anniversary (April 2026).
  • Anthropic, Block and OpenAI co-founded the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation, contributing MCP, goose and AGENTS.md as founding projects, backed by Google, Microsoft, AWS, Cloudflare and Bloomberg (December 2025).
  • Salesforce shipped Slackbot as a general-availability agent (January 2026), then added 30-plus AI features including a desktop agent and an MCP client reaching Agentforce, Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, Notion, Workday and ServiceNow, running on Claude (March 2026).
  • Microsoft made Entra Agent ID generally available, issuing AI agents first-class directory identities rather than borrowed human credentials (April 2026).
  • OpenAI launched Workspace Agents with per-user, per-agent memory and team ownership, deprecating Custom GPTs for business, enterprise and education accounts (April 2026).
  • Microsoft made Work IQ generally available at Build as a Data, Memory and Inference layer over Microsoft 365, alongside Agent 365 for administering which MCP servers agents may reach (June 2026).
  • Google consolidated Agentspace and Vertex into the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform and shipped Agent Engine Sessions and Memory Bank for persistent agent context (2026).
  • Glean raised a $150M Series F at a $7.2B valuation on roughly $300M ARR, after crossing 250 million agentic actions (2026).
  • Block launched Buzz, an Apache-2.0, model-agnostic chat workspace in which agents are members holding portable cryptographic identities (July 2026).
  • MCP shipped its 2026-07-28 revision, the largest in its history: a stateless request/response core, Tasks moved out to an extension, and close to half a billion SDK downloads a month across more than 10,000 public servers (July 2026).

One pattern in that list is worth stating separately, because it was not visible in the 2024–2025 evidence. Four of the largest competitors in this market each took the layer they had built and gave it away to a neutral foundation. That is not generosity and it is not a truce. It is what a market does when it agrees the interface is not the prize.

Inference

From this evidence, the following conclusions can be drawn:

  • Every major AI platform is investing in mechanisms for persistent organizational context.
  • These mechanisms serve the same purpose despite very different architectures, naming, and packaging. A wire protocol and a text file checked into a repository are not built alike, but they are doing the same job.
  • The investment is accelerating, not decelerating.
  • The investments are independent of model architecture, they solve a different problem than intelligence.

Prediction

If organizational cognition is a useful framework, it predicts:

  1. Enterprise AI procurement will increasingly evaluate platforms on context accumulation and persistence, not just model quality.
  2. Switching costs between AI platforms will rise before they fall. They rise while organizations accumulate cognitive assets that have no shared representation, and fall only once one emerges.
  3. New enterprise roles will emerge: cognitive architects, AI governance leads, context engineers.
  4. Standards for cognitive portability (export, version control, merge) will become a competitive battleground.
  5. The most valuable AI platforms will be those that accumulate organizational cognition fastest, not those with the highest benchmark scores.

These predictions are falsifiable. The next section specifies how.

8. This Framework Is Wrong If…

A framework that cannot be disproven is not a framework. It is a vibe.

Three conditions would disprove the organizational cognition thesis. They test different things: whether the mechanism works, whether it accumulates into something costly to abandon, and whether it survives better models. Any one of them is sufficient.

Persistent organizational context does not produce measurably better decisions. If AI platforms with memory, connectors, and custom instructions produce the same decision quality as a stateless model handed the same documents, then accumulating context buys nothing and the entire product convergence documented above is wasted effort. This is the test of the mechanism. It can be run today, on a single team, with a held-out set of real decisions.

Enterprises switch AI providers without meaningful organizational cost. The strongest test, and the sharpest. If a Fortune 500 company replaces its primary AI platform after two years of heavy use and reports that the transition was primarily a data migration problem, comparable to switching cloud providers or databases, then organizational cognition is not a durable asset. What would confirm it instead is a switch that costs months of degraded decision quality that no amount of data export prevents.

A stateless model with superior reasoning eliminates the need for accumulated context. If a future model produces equivalent or better organizational decisions given only current documents and a prompt, with no learned history of how the organization behaves, then organizational cognition is a transitional artifact of a weak generation of models rather than a structural layer. The moat dissolves when intelligence crosses a threshold.

The second is the one I would actually watch, and the one that would change my mind fastest. If a large enterprise with two years of heavy use moves platforms and the switching cost turns out to be a line item in a migration budget rather than a loss of capability, then there is no new layer here and I should stop describing one. The first would tell me the mechanism does not work. The third would tell me it works but does not last. I am not reserving the right to reweigh evidence until the framework survives: any of the three, observed clearly, is enough to abandon it.

9. Open Questions

This framework is the beginning of an inquiry, not the end of one. Several questions remain unresolved.

Who owns organizational cognition? When an organization's decision-making patterns are externalized into an AI platform, who holds the rights? The enterprise that generated the patterns? The platform that hosts them? The employees whose interactions produced them? This is not a legal question that has been settled. It may become one of the defining intellectual property questions of the next decade.

Can organizational cognition be exported? Today, switching AI platforms means losing context. Is that a temporary condition or a structural one? If organizational cognition can be exported in a standard format and imported into a competing platform, the lock-in argument weakens substantially. If it cannot, if the patterns are inextricably tied to the platform's architecture, then lock-in is structural.

2026 answered a narrower version of this question and left the real one open. Interfaces standardized fast: MCP for tools, A2A for agents, AGENTS.md for written conventions, all three now under neutral governance, and Buzz arguing that even identity should be portable. What did not standardize is the accumulation. There is no export format for the memory in Work IQ, the Memory Bank in Gemini Enterprise, a workspace agent's per-agent history, or Glean's permissions-aware graph, and no vendor has proposed one. The industry has converged on how to reach organizational cognition while leaving what it learns firmly inside the platform. That may be a sequencing problem, or it may be the answer.

Can it be version-controlled? Organizations evolve. An approval path that made sense in 2024 may be wrong in 2026. Can organizational cognition be branched, merged, rolled back, and audited the way code can? Or does it accumulate as an ever-growing, increasingly stale representation of how the organization used to work?

Can it be merged? When two organizations merge, their formal systems (ERP, CRM, HRIS) are consolidated through a defined process. Their organizational cognition is not. What happens when two sets of accumulated AI context, two different ways of making decisions, need to be reconciled? This is a problem that does not yet exist but will.

Can it decay? If organizational cognition accumulates through continuous interaction, what happens when interaction stops? Does it persist as a static asset? Does it degrade? Does it become actively misleading, representing an organization that no longer exists? Memory without learning is not memory. It is residue.

Should enterprises treat it like intellectual property? Organizations spend millions building knowledge bases, training programs, and documentation. Organizational cognition may prove more valuable than all three combined. Should it be actively managed, audited, and protected? Or does it emerge as a byproduct of tool usage and resist deliberate cultivation?

Can it become a system of record? Databases became systems of record for organizational data. CRMs became systems of record for customer relationships. Could an AI platform become the system of record for organizational decision-making: the authoritative source for how the organization thinks? If so, that platform's position in the enterprise architecture is fundamentally different from "a chatbot we use."

These questions do not have answers today.

But if organizational cognition proves to be the durable asset this framework suggests, they will be among the most consequential questions in enterprise technology.

The companies that answer them first, whether incumbents building platforms or startups building tools, may define the next generation of enterprise software.

Not by building the smartest model.

By building the best infrastructure for how organizations think.
